Where is SEO heading in 2027, and do ordinary websites have a future?

Lately, I have been reading quite a lot about changes in SEO, analysing search results, observing my own projects and trying to understand where this industry is heading.

Oleksandr Padalko24/11/20269 min read

I recently came across an interesting article about the future of affiliate SEO. Although much of it focused on affiliate marketing, I believe some of the issues it raised are relevant to almost any business promoting itself online.

What interests me most is how the value of a website is changing as website development and content production become increasingly accessible, while dependence on search engines remains relatively high.

Between a thousand websites and one major brand

There are currently two very different approaches to SEO in affiliate marketing.

Some teams launch hundreds, sometimes thousands, of small websites every month. They automate development and content generation, purchase domains with an established history and experiment with search results.

They are not particularly concerned about how long an individual website will survive. If it generates revenue before losing its rankings, it has served its purpose. This business model relies on the number of projects launched and their combined profitability.

Others spend years developing a single brand. They build their own media platforms and mobile applications, develop social media channels and establish a loyal audience.

I am more interested in the future of those who find themselves somewhere between these two approaches. Ordinary businesses whose owners do not have multimillion-dollar budgets and have no intention of launching a thousand domains every month.

Technical service centres, online stores, language schools, construction companies and beauty salons. For them, a website is a tool for attracting customers rather than an independent source of revenue.

Automation and the declining value of mass-produced content

Just a few years ago, building a large informational website required considerable resources. You had to find writers, prepare content briefs, conduct keyword research, and write and edit hundreds of articles.

Today, someone with the right technical skills can automate most of this process.

Take n8n, for example, a platform I actively work with. Even without advanced programming skills, it is possible to build a relatively simple content automation workflow.

We create a Google Sheets spreadsheet containing future article topics, keywords, publication languages and additional content requirements.

We then configure the following steps in n8n:

  1. Schedule Trigger: starts the workflow according to a predefined schedule.
  2. Google Sheets: retrieves the article topic and keywords.
  3. HTTP Request: collects information from selected sources or through a search API.
  4. AI / LLM: analyses the collected material and prepares the article.
  5. SEO Processing: prepares the title, meta description and internal linking.
  6. Image Generation: creates a cover image.
  7. WordPress / CMS: saves the completed material as a draft.
  8. Telegram: sends a notification that the article is ready for review.

If necessary, the workflow can also include multilingual translations, structured data generation, image optimisation and link validation.

With the appropriate experience, a basic version of this automation can be assembled in an evening. It will require occasional maintenance, but most routine operations will be performed automatically.

The same approach can be used to create landing pages, populate product catalogues, prepare product descriptions and automate individual stages of website development.

Since these technologies are available to almost everyone, the ability to produce large volumes of content quickly is gradually ceasing to be a competitive advantage.

However, I disagree with the idea that written content is losing its importance for SEO. What is losing value is primarily second-hand information that can be reproduced on virtually any other website without much effort.

For example, when working on a website for a laser equipment service centre, it is possible to produce dozens of general articles about why diode lasers overheat. There is plenty of information on this subject online.

Alternatively, we can describe an actual repair. Show the equipment, explain the fault, describe the diagnostic process, present measurement results and demonstrate the work carried out.

Artificial intelligence can help present this information as well, but the information must first be obtained through real work.

That is why I see little point in endless debates about whether an article was written by a human or by artificial intelligence. I am more interested in where the information comes from, how reliable it is and whether it provides practical value.

Business dependence on search engines

The extent to which businesses depend on Google has concerned me for some time.

A website owner can spend years investing in its development, working on technical optimisation, improving content and acquiring backlinks. Then another algorithm update takes place, and part of the organic traffic disappears.

Meanwhile, the business itself may not have changed at all. The quality of its services remains the same, employees continue doing their jobs, and customers are satisfied.

The only thing that has changed is how an external system evaluates its website.

From a business owner's perspective, this is a rather strange situation. You invest your own money in developing a company, yet a significant portion of the results depends on decisions made by another company over which you have practically no influence.

The article I read presented the example of Tribuna.com, a sports media platform. What interested me was not so much the scale of the project as its approach to building an audience. People use its mobile applications, follow its social media channels, return to its website and search directly for the brand name.

A business like this does not need to introduce itself to its customers through Google every single time.

Naturally, comparing a large sports media platform with a small company in Wrocław or Kyiv would not be entirely appropriate. Nevertheless, the same principle can be applied to small businesses.

If someone searches for an equipment repair service today, finds a company through Google, uses its services and searches for that company's name the next time, I consider that a successful marketing outcome.

That is why, when analysing website statistics, I am interested not only in improving rankings for commercial keywords but also in changes in branded search queries. For me, this is one indication that people are beginning to recognise the company.

How artificial intelligence is changing customer acquisition

Previously, users entered a search query, received a list of websites, opened several pages and compared the information themselves.

Today, they can increasingly receive a ready-made answer without visiting a website.

For informational websites, this is potentially a serious problem, particularly for those whose business models depend on advertising impressions and visitor numbers.

The situation is somewhat different for companies that provide real-world services.

Artificial intelligence can explain why a laser device is overheating. However, it cannot visit the customer, diagnose the equipment or repair its cooling system.

The customer will still need to find a specialist. Instead of browsing dozens of websites independently, they may simply ask an AI assistant to recommend a particular company.

When working on my own projects, I already pay attention to how information about companies is presented to artificial intelligence systems. I am interested in which pages are used as sources, whether service information is interpreted correctly and whether companies appear in responses to relevant queries.

There is still considerable uncertainty in this area. There is no universal set of instructions that guarantees ChatGPT or Google will recommend a particular business.

Nevertheless, the fact that users can discover a company without visiting its website requires us to assess the results of search engine optimisation somewhat differently.

The number of clicks no longer necessarily reflects the full picture of a company's online presence.

What I would focus on in 2027

I do not think small businesses need to replicate the model of a large informational website.

If a company repairs equipment in Poland, it does not need to publish ten thousand articles about cosmetology. It makes considerably more sense to describe its own services, the equipment it works with, its geographical coverage and actual repair cases in detail.

At the same time, I would continue working on several areas:

  • Traditional SEO, technical optimisation, keyword research and backlink profiles.
  • Publishing original expert content and completed projects.
  • Brand development, social media and customer reviews.
  • Company visibility in responses generated by AI-powered search systems.
  • Automation of routine processes and reduction of operating costs.
  • Analysis of genuine customer enquiries, sales and the effectiveness of acquisition channels.

The last point is particularly important to me.

I would much rather receive ten genuine enquiries from potential customers than ten thousand visits from people who have no need for the company's services.

An attractive graph in Google Analytics does not, by itself, pay office rent, employee salaries or other business expenses.

My thoughts on the future of SEO

I do not think SEO is dying. Although its death is announced so regularly that it could almost be considered an industry tradition.

As a developer and SEO specialist, I appreciate the opportunities that automation provides today. Small teams can perform work that was previously only accessible to large companies.

However, automating website development and automating the creation of a successful business are still two different things.

Perhaps in a few years we will spend considerably less time discussing individual keyword rankings and considerably more time discussing a company's overall online presence.

I do not know exactly what search results will look like at the end of 2027. I doubt anyone currently selling SEO services knows for certain either.

But as website development and content production become increasingly inexpensive, I see more value in developing things that are harder to copy or generate automatically: professional experience, reputation, customer relationships and brand recognition.

In my opinion, these should form the foundation of a long-term SEO strategy for an ordinary business.
