Three SEO Rules I Would Not Ignore

When working on websites, there is always something that could be improved.

Oleksandr Padalko17/11/20265 min read

A URL could be shorter. The navigation could be cleaner. A heading could be rewritten. An entire service page could be redesigned.

From a developer's perspective, these changes often make sense.

Cleaner code, better structure and a more modern interface are all good things.

But SEO introduces another consideration.

A page that looks technically imperfect may already be bringing customers from Google.

Changing it without understanding its current performance can create unnecessary problems.

That is why I follow three practical rules.

Rule One. Do Not Change URLs Without a Good Reason

Imagine a service company operating in Wrocław.

One of its pages uses the address /services/repair-lasers/ and regularly receives impressions, clicks and customer enquiries.

During a website redesign, someone suggests changing it to /laser-repair-wroclaw/.

The new address looks more descriptive.

But is that enough reason to change it?

The existing URL may already have a history in Google's index. Other websites may link to it, and internal links may point to the same address.

After a URL change, search engines need to process the redirect and update their understanding of the page.

A correctly implemented 301 redirect helps transfer signals from the old address to the new one.

However, it does not guarantee that visibility will remain completely stable throughout the migration.

Changing hundreds of URLs without a proper redirect map is particularly risky.

When a URL Change Is Necessary

Sometimes there are valid reasons.

A company may be merging websites, restructuring its services or resolving serious duplication problems.

In these cases, I would prepare a mapping of old and new URLs, implement direct permanent redirects and update internal links, canonical tags and XML sitemaps.

After deployment, server responses and indexing should be monitored through Google Search Console.

But I would not change a successful URL simply because another version looks better.

Rule Two. Do Not Rewrite Successful Pages Without Data

This principle applies to both SEO and web development.

Imagine a service page that consistently attracts organic visitors and generates enquiries.

During an audit, someone suggests shortening the title, rewriting the content and rearranging several sections.

The updated page might perform better.

But it might also lose visibility for some of its existing queries.

The issue is not that changes are bad.

The issue is making changes without knowing what problem they are supposed to solve.

Before editing a successful page, I want to understand which queries generate impressions, which ones bring clicks and whether those visitors contribute to business goals.

If a page receives traffic but generates few enquiries, the problem may involve the offer, contact form or user experience.

If the page converts well but has limited search visibility, the priorities will be different.

Make Changes You Can Evaluate

Whenever possible, I prefer controlled improvements.

Rewriting the entire page, changing the H1, modifying the URL and rebuilding internal links at the same time makes it difficult to understand what influenced the outcome.

A better approach is to record the initial performance, define a hypothesis, implement the change and evaluate the results over a meaningful period.

Critical technical errors and security issues should still be fixed promptly.

But changing something merely because it can be changed is not a strategy.

Rule Three. Study Competitors Without Copying Them

Competitor analysis is an important part of SEO planning.

It helps reveal how other companies structure their services, what topics they cover and which questions they answer.

However, a website ranking well today did not necessarily achieve its position because of its current layout.

It may have spent years earning links, building brand recognition and publishing useful content.

Copying its navigation, headings and article lengths does not mean a new website will achieve similar results.

Which Competitors Should You Study?

I would not focus only on the largest companies in the market.

It can also be useful to examine relatively new websites that are currently improving their organic visibility.

They may reveal interesting approaches to content and site structure in the current competitive environment.

But correlation is not proof of causation.

If a website grows after launching a blog, that does not automatically mean the blog caused the growth.

Other factors may include new links, technical improvements, changes in demand or additional content.

The purpose of competitor research is to understand opportunities, not to reproduce somebody else's website.

Why These Rules Matter During a Redesign

A redesign is often treated as a visual project.

For SEO, it can be a significant technical change.

During migration, it is easy to modify URLs, remove valuable content, break internal links or introduce incorrect canonical tags.

The new website may look much better while receiving less organic traffic.

That is why I recommend identifying important pages before beginning a redesign.

We need to know which URLs already receive search traffic, which content performs well and which changes are genuinely necessary.

What I Would Check Before a Major Website Update

I would begin with Google Search Console.

I would review impressions, clicks, queries and the pages that currently attract organic visitors.

Then I would examine conversion data and identify the most valuable landing pages.

Only after that would I prepare a migration or optimization plan.

If a URL needs to change, it requires a redirect. If content is being rewritten, important topics should not disappear accidentally. If navigation is changing, internal linking needs to remain logical.

After deployment, the website should be checked again.

Final Thoughts

SEO does not mean constantly rebuilding everything.

Sometimes the best decision is to preserve something that already works.

My three rules are straightforward.

Do not change successful URLs without a valid reason.

Do not optimize working pages without understanding their performance.

Do not copy competitors without understanding why they attract traffic.

Good SEO is not measured by how many changes appear in a technical report.

It is measured by whether those changes help people find useful information and help businesses attract customers.
