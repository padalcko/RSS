3 SEO Mistakes That Cost Your Website Google Traffic and Customers

Imagine spending money on a new website.

Oleksandr Padalko20/11/20266 min read

You pay for the design, development, content, and eventually SEO.

The website loads quickly. It works on mobile devices. Google can crawl and index its pages.

But hardly any customers arrive through organic search.

So you start looking for an explanation.

Did Google change its algorithm? Did competitors build more backlinks? Do you need another twenty blog posts?

Sometimes the problem is much simpler.

Your website might be technically sound while failing to explain clearly what your business offers and which searches its pages should answer.

Here are three common SEO mistakes worth investigating before increasing your marketing budget.

Mistake #1. Your page titles don't match how customers search

Imagine an online clothing store.

Its navigation includes a category called "Boys".

Clicking it opens a page containing children's jackets, trousers, and T-shirts.

That makes perfect sense to someone browsing the store.

But what if the content management system automatically generates a page title like "Buy Boys London"?

That doesn't sound like something a customer would search for.

People are much more likely to use phrases such as "boys' clothing", "boys' winter jackets", or a specific product name.

The category label makes sense inside the website navigation, but loses its meaning when presented without context.

Service businesses often make the same mistake.

A company repairs professional cosmetic laser equipment, yet its landing page is simply called "Technical Services".

Technical services for what?

Cars? Computers? Air conditioning systems?

"Cosmetic Laser Repair Services" communicates the subject much more clearly.

Technical terminology versus customer language

Business owners naturally use industry terminology.

After all, they work with it every day.

But customers may describe the same service using completely different words.

For example, a technician might refer to "paintless dent repair", while a driver searches for "car dent removal without painting".

Both expressions may be relevant, but their popularity and search intent can differ.

That's why keyword selection shouldn't be based on assumptions.

Use Google Search Console, examine actual search results, and compare the language people use when looking for your services.

And remember that the keyword with the highest search volume isn't automatically the best choice.

If it doesn't accurately describe your service, attracting more impressions may not lead to more customers.

What should you check?

Open your most important landing pages.

Read their Title tags and H1 headings.

Would someone unfamiliar with your business immediately understand what the page offers?

Does the wording match how potential customers search?

If not, that's a good place to start.

Mistake #2. Your pages compete against each other

SEO specialists call this keyword cannibalization.

The term sounds dramatic, but the idea is straightforward.

Imagine an automotive repair business with two pages:

"Car Body Repair Services"

"Vehicle Bodywork Repairs"

Both pages describe essentially the same services.

Both target similar searches.

Google may display one page for a particular query and then show the other.

The business owner notices fluctuating rankings without understanding the cause.

This can be a symptom of keyword cannibalization.

However, similar keywords don't automatically mean that two pages are competing.

For example, "car body repair" and "paintless dent removal" can represent different services and different customer needs.

Both pages may deserve to exist.

How can you identify the problem?

Open Google Search Console.

Select an important search query and examine which URLs receive impressions and clicks.

If multiple pages repeatedly appear for the same query, investigate their content and purpose.

Are they answering the same question?

If so, consolidating the content might make sense.

If they cover different services, make those differences clearer through headings, content, and internal links.

Sometimes a small content update is enough.

In other cases, merging pages and implementing a 301 redirect may be appropriate.

The important thing is not to delete pages simply because their keywords look similar.

Don't forget technical duplicates

URL configuration can create additional problems.

For example:

example.com/service

example.com/service/

A server may treat these as different addresses.

If both serve identical content with a 200 response, review canonical tags, redirects, and indexing.

The same principle applies to HTTP versus HTTPS and www versus non-www versions.

Your website should consistently use its preferred URL structure.

Mistake #3. Your page contains plenty of content but very little useful information

Imagine visiting an online flower shop.

You open its "Tulips" category.

The title is correct. The products look attractive.

But the page is filled with extensive navigation, promotions for roses, gift packages, and unrelated sections.

Does that automatically mean Google will think the page is about roses?

No.

Modern search engines can distinguish navigation from main content.

However, when the actual page content is weak, excessive secondary information may make its purpose less clear.

The solution isn't to repeat "tulips" fifty times.

The solution is to make the page genuinely useful to someone looking for tulips.

A practical service-business example

You visit a page titled "Diode Laser Repair".

It contains several paragraphs about innovation, professionalism, outstanding quality, and years of experience.

Everything sounds impressive.

But you still don't know what the company repairs.

Does it diagnose faults? Repair handpieces? Service cooling systems? Replace damaged components?

How do you request a repair?

These are the questions a potential customer actually wants answered.

A useful landing page explains the service instead of relying entirely on generic marketing language.

How do you improve it?

Read the page from a customer's perspective.

Remove unnecessary repetition.

Explain what the service includes.

Answer common questions.

Use clear headings and relevant internal links.

You don't need to repeat your primary keyword in every paragraph.

You need content that matches the visitor's intent and helps them make a decision.

A simple SEO audit you can perform yourself

You don't necessarily need an expensive audit to identify the first problems.

Start with five steps.

  1. Open Google Search Console and find pages with impressions but relatively few clicks.
  2. Compare their Title tags and H1 headings with actual search queries.
  3. Look for pages that serve essentially the same purpose.
  4. Check canonical URLs, redirects, and potential duplicate addresses.
  5. Read your most important landing pages as if you were a first-time customer.

Avoid changing everything at once.

Identify a problem, make a focused improvement, and monitor the results.

SEO takes time.

Changing a heading today doesn't guarantee a top-three ranking tomorrow.

But clear content and a well-organized website provide a much stronger foundation for organic growth.

Final thoughts

Businesses often spend money on backlinks, new articles, and additional SEO campaigns while overlooking the pages they already have.

Sometimes the most valuable improvements involve clearer service names, better website structure, and more useful information.
